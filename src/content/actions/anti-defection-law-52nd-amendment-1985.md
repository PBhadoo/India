facts:
  - The Constitution (Fifty-second Amendment) Act, 1985 (Bill No. 22 of 1985) added the Tenth Schedule and amended Articles 101, 102, 190 and 191; it came into force on a date notified by the Central Government [1]. Secondary sources give 1 March 1985 as that date.
  - Under the original text, a member elected on a party ticket is disqualified if he voluntarily gives up party membership, or votes or abstains contrary to a party direction without prior permission; an independent who joins a party after election is also disqualified [1].
  - The original text exempted a group of at least one-third of a legislature party on a split (paragraph 3) and treated a merger as valid if two-thirds of the legislature party agreed (paragraph 4) [1]. A 2003 amendment later changed the merger/split provisions [2].
  - The presiding officer of the House decides disqualification questions [1].
  - In Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992) the Supreme Court upheld the Tenth Schedule, struck down paragraph 7 (which barred court jurisdiction), and held the presiding officer's decision open to limited judicial review [3][4].
  - In Keisham Meghachandra Singh v. Speaker, Manipur Legislative Assembly (2020), the Supreme Court held that the Speaker, acting as a tribunal, must decide disqualification petitions within a reasonable period, generally three months [3].
  - The phrase "Aaya Ram, Gaya Ram" refers to Haryana MLA Gaya Lal, who changed party three times in fifteen days in 1967 [5].

## In depth

Before 1985 the Constitution did not regulate legislators who changed parties. The Tenth Schedule introduced rules for disqualification, with the Speaker or Chairman deciding. The original text allowed a split by one-third of a legislature party and a merger by two-thirds; a 2003 amendment changed those provisions.

In 1992 the Supreme Court in Kihoto Hollohan upheld the Schedule and held that presiding officers' decisions can be reviewed in limited circumstances. In 2020 it set a general three-month norm for deciding petitions.

Supporters of the law argue that it provides stability for elected governments and curbs bargaining over legislators. Critics argue that it restricts dissent within parties, that decisions by presiding officers have sometimes been delayed, and that its operation depends on how presiding officers act.
