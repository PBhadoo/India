facts:
  - Parliament enacted the Armed Forces (Assam and Manipur) Special Powers Act in 1958, after an ordinance promulgated by the President on 22 May 1958; secondary sources give 11 September 1958 as the date of the Act [1][3].
  - The Naga Hills, then part of Assam, was the first area to which the Act was applied; it was later adapted to Arunachal Pradesh, Meghalaya, Mizoram and Nagaland [1].
  - Section 3 allows an area to be notified as "disturbed". Section 4 allows an officer in a disturbed area, after giving due warning, to fire upon or use force "even to the causing of death" for the maintenance of public order, and to arrest without warrant and search premises [1].
  - Section 6 bars prosecution or other legal proceedings against persons acting under the Act except with the previous sanction of the Central Government [1].
  - In Naga People's Movement of Human Rights v. Union of India ((1998) 2 SCC 109) the Supreme Court upheld the Act's constitutional validity and added safeguards, including the Army's "Dos and Don'ts" [1].
  - The Committee to Review AFSPA (Justice B. P. Jeevan Reddy), reporting in 2005, recommended that the Act be repealed and that suitable provisions be inserted in the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967 [1].
  - On 8 July 2016 the Supreme Court held that armed forces do not have blanket immunity from prosecution for alleged excesses in AFSPA areas (Extra Judicial Execution Victim Families Association v. Union of India) [3].

## In depth

The Act allows the Centre or a Governor to notify an area as disturbed, after which commissioned and non-commissioned officers of the armed forces may use force, arrest without a warrant and enter and search premises. Prosecution of those acting under the Act requires central sanction.

The Supreme Court upheld the Act in 1998 and added safeguards. A 2005 review committee under Justice B. P. Jeevan Reddy recommended repeal and suggested placing provisions in the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act. The same report recorded the views of the Army, the Ministry of Home Affairs and other forces, who argued for retention, and the views of many people and groups in the north-east who argued for repeal. In 2016 the Supreme Court held that immunity is not absolute.

This entry records the positions in the 2005 report and later court decisions. It does not include year-by-year security data, which were not reviewed. Evidence on how far the Act affected insurgency or civilian harm is therefore limited, and confidence in any outcome scoring should be low.
