  - The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita Bill was introduced on 11 August 2023, examined by the Standing Committee on Home Affairs and withdrawn; the Bharatiya Nyaya (Second) Sanhita Bill was introduced on 12 December 2023 [2].
  - The Lok Sabha passed it on 20 December 2023 and the Rajya Sabha on 21 December 2023; the President's assent was dated 25 December 2023 (Act No. 45 of 2023) [1][5].
  - The BNS and the companion Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita and Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam came into effect on 1 July 2024 [3].
  - The BNS has 358 sections in 20 chapters; it adds 20 offences, drops 19 IPC provisions, raises imprisonment for 33 offences and fines for 83, introduces mandatory minimum punishment for 23 and community service for six [5].
  - It removes sedition as an offence and adds Section 152, "Act endangering sovereignty, unity and integrity of India"; it adds organised crime, terrorism, and murder or grievous hurt by a group on grounds of race, caste or community [1][2][3][5].
  - It retains the marital rape exception and omits IPC Section 377 [2].
  - Under the companion procedure code, the permitted period of police custody can extend up to 90 days, against a 15-day limit under Section 167(2) of the CrPC [3].

    evidence: PRS notes that several offences overlap with special laws that carry different penalties or procedures, which may lead to multiple charges [2].

## In depth

The BNS replaces the Indian Penal Code of 1860. PRS describes it as largely retaining IPC provisions while adding some offences, removing offences struck down by courts and raising some penalties. It came into force together with a new procedure code and evidence law.

The government described the reform as replacing colonial-era laws with laws framed by Indians. It stated that the procedure code would be victim-centric and set tighter trial timelines. Critics, including the authors of one analysis in The Wire, argue that the BNS keeps or expands state powers and does not resolve long-standing criticisms such as the marital rape exception. The new Section 152 replaces the word sedition; commentators disagree on how far its standard differs.

Because the code came into force in July 2024, little outcome data exist. This entry therefore records design features and stated reasons, and its confidence is low. It does not assess whether prosecution or conviction rates have changed.
