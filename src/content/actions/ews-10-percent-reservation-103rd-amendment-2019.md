facts:
- Parliament enacted the Constitution (One Hundred and Third Amendment) Act, 2019 on 9 January 2019; the President gave assent on 12 January 2019, and the Supreme Court records it as in effect from 14 January 2019 [1][2].
- The amendment inserted Articles 15(6) and 16(6), enabling the State to make special provision, including reservation of up to 10% in addition to existing reservations, for 'any economically weaker section of citizens' in educational institutions and public appointments; it does not apply to classes covered by Articles 15(4), 15(5) and 16(4) (SC, ST and socially and educationally backward classes) [1].
- 'On 7 November 2022 a five-judge Constitution Bench upheld the amendment by 3:2: Justices Dinesh Maheshwari, Bela M. Trivedi and J. B. Pardiwala for; Chief Justice U. U. Lalit and Justice S. Ravindra Bhat against [1]. A review petition was dismissed on 9 May 2023 [2].'
- The majority held that reservation based on economic criteria alone does not violate the basic structure, that exclusion of SC, ST and OBC classes is permissible, and that the 50% ceiling is not inflexible and applies only to reservations under Articles 15(4), 15(5) and 16(4) [1].
- The minority held the amendment violates the equality code and the basic structure because the exclusion of the poorest among SC, ST and backward classes is discriminatory; Justice Bhat held economic criteria per se are permissible under Article 15 but not Article 16 [1].

## In depth

The amendment was passed in January 2019. It lets the Centre and States reserve up to 10% of seats and posts for economically weaker sections who are not covered by the existing SC, ST and OBC reservations.

The Supreme Court decided the challenge in November 2022. The three-judge majority reasoned that affirmative action can be based on economic criteria, that excluding groups already covered is part of targeting a new group, and that the 50% limit is not rigid. The two dissenting judges reasoned that the exclusion of the poorest among SC, ST and OBC communities amounts to discrimination that violates the basic structure.

The sources reviewed give the legal reasoning in detail but no national figures on how many seats or posts were filled under the quota or who benefited. Any assessment of outcomes therefore depends on data not located here, and the entry's confidence rating is low.

Supporters point to the Statement of Objects and Reasons and to the Court's majority view. Opponents point to the minority judgment, to the 1992 Indra Sawhney framework, and to the effect of the additional quota on total reservation levels.
