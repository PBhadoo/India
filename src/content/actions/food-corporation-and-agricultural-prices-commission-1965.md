## In depth

The two institutions were set up within weeks of each other in early 1965. The Agricultural Prices Commission (later renamed) advises on minimum support prices; the Food Corporation of India buys grain at those prices, stores it and supplies the public distribution system. Together with the new seeds introduced from 1966, they are described in the committee summary as a combined technology and price policy.

Official data show procurement and public distribution growing from a few million tonnes in the mid-1960s to tens of millions by the 2010s. The system now underpins the food-security law and free grain schemes.

The 2015 High Level Committee, appointed by the government of the day, evaluated the FCI against its three original objectives. It concluded that price support reached a small share of farmers, that leakages in public distribution were large, and that stocks often exceeded norms. Its recommendations, including cash transfers and a smaller food-law coverage, were contested by others, who argued that procurement also supports market prices indirectly and that grain distribution protects poorer households. The 6% figure is drawn from NSSO survey data on sales to procurement agencies, so it measures direct sales only; the committee summary itself notes that indirect gains are debated.

The institutions have continued, with changes of name and mandate, under all governments since 1965.
