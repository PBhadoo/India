## In depth

The programme combined three elements: new seed varieties, assured procurement at announced prices, and more irrigation and fertiliser. Its reach was concentrated at first in wheat in the north-west, where irrigation was assured, and later extended to rice.

Official statistics show a large increase in foodgrain output between the early 1970s and 1990 and a move from net imports to roughly balanced trade in grain by the early 1970s. The statistics cannot separate the seed programme from other changes of the period, such as the expansion of irrigation, fertiliser use and support prices, and from weather variation. The 1972-74 period shows that output could still fall sharply.

Later assessments focus on the long-run costs in the regions that adopted the package. Reports cited by Down To Earth describe falling productivity growth, declining water tables and salinity in Punjab and Haryana. The authors and officials quoted there generally do not dispute the output gains; they dispute whether the input-intensive model was sustainable and whether it should have been broader in crops and regions.

Supporters point to the change in the national food position within a decade. Critics point to the concentration of benefits and the environmental costs. Both readings draw on the same output and input data, and differ mainly on which time horizon and which regions are given weight.
