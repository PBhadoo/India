## In depth

After the 1998 tests, India remained outside the NPT and faced restrictions on nuclear trade. The 2005 framework proposed that India separate its civilian and military facilities, accept IAEA safeguards on the civilian side, and in return gain access to international civil nuclear commerce. Implementation required US law (the Hyde Act), an India-specific IAEA safeguards agreement, an NSG waiver and a bilateral 123 Agreement.

Domestically the deal became the subject of a confidence vote after the Left Front withdrew support. The government stated that it protected India's strategic programme; the Left and some analysts argued that the Hyde Act's conditions and the NSG waiver's language could constrain testing and foreign policy.

Outcomes are mixed and slow. Nuclear capacity was 8.78 GW in 2024-25, about 3.1% of generation, and foreign-supplied reactor projects were affected by India's 2010 liability law. Supporters point to access to fuel and the diplomatic effect; critics point to limited capacity growth and the costs of the political settlement. Some dates, such as the IAEA Board date, differ between sources.
