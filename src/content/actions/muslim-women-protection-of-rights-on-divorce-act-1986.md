  - "On 23 April 1985 a five-judge Constitution Bench led by CJI Y.V. Chandrachud decided Mohd. Ahmed Khan v. Shah Bano Begum. It held that a divorced Muslim wife who has not remarried and cannot maintain herself can claim maintenance under Section 125 CrPC, which applies irrespective of religion, and found no real conflict with Muslim personal law, citing Quranic verses 241-242. The Court also observed that Article 44 (uniform civil code) remained unimplemented [1]."
  - "The Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act, 1986 is Act No. 25 of 1986 and is dated 19 May 1986 on India Code [2]. The Bill was introduced in the Lok Sabha in early May 1986 (sources give 5 May) after a long debate [3]."
  - "The Act provides that a divorced Muslim woman is entitled to a 'reasonable and fair provision and maintenance' from her former husband within the iddat period; if she cannot maintain herself afterwards, a Magistrate may direct her relatives or the State Wakf Board to pay; Section 125 CrPC applies only if both parties agree in writing, as summarised [3]."
  - "Arif Mohammad Khan, then a Minister of State, resigned from the Council of Ministers in protest against the Bill [3][5]."
  - "In Danial Latifi v. Union of India (28 September 2001) a five-judge Bench upheld the Act as not violating Articles 14 and 21, and read 'reasonable and fair provision and maintenance' as covering the wife's future needs, to be arranged by the husband within the iddat period, so his liability is not confined to the iddat period [4]."
  - "In Mohd. Abdul Samad v. State of Telangana (10 July 2024, 2024 INSC 506) Justices B.V. Nagarathna and A.G. Masih, in separate concurring opinions, held that a divorced Muslim woman can seek maintenance under Section 125 CrPC and that the 1986 Act does not displace it [6]."

## In depth

The Act responded to Shah Bano, in which the Supreme Court applied the criminal-law maintenance provision to a divorced Muslim woman. The government of the time introduced a Bill that limited the husband's liability under the new statute to the iddat period and moved later support to relatives and the Wakf Board. Supporters, including Muslim organisations, described the judgment as an intrusion on personal law; critics, including a minister who resigned and women's groups, said the Bill reduced a woman's protection.

The Supreme Court's 2001 ruling in Danial Latifi upheld the Act but read its text to require provision for the woman's future needs, which supporters of the Act and its critics each cite as vindicating their view. In 2024 the Court confirmed that Section 125 remains available as an option.

The Statement of Objects and Reasons, the Lok Sabha debate and the exact passage date were not retrieved; the date shown is the date on India Code.
