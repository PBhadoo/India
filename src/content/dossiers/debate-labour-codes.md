## In depth

India's labour laws are in the Concurrent List, so both the Centre and states legislate on them. Four codes (wages, industrial relations, social security, occupational safety, health and working conditions) were passed in 2019 and 2020 to replace 29 central Acts, and the Government notified them as in force on 21 November 2025 [1][3]. Central and state rules are being notified in stages; as of January 2026, 32 states and union territories had published draft rules [1].

The debate is whether to keep the codes as notified, amend them or repeal them. The Government and the Economic Survey describe the codes as a tripartitely deliberated simplification that extends protection to gig and platform workers, requires appointment letters, gives fixed-term workers equal benefits and sets a national floor wage, with projected gains in formalisation and employment [1]. Central trade unions, including the Congress-affiliated INTUC, call for repeal; the INC says the codes repackage existing laws without delivering wage and social-security demands [5][6]. PRS, a non-partisan research body, flagged higher thresholds for retrenchment permission and contract labour and the large share of matters left to rules [3][4].

The idea of consolidating labour laws has a long history. The Second National Commission on Labour (2002, appointed by the BJP-led Vajpayee government) recommended grouping laws into broad groups; a parliamentary committee in 2009, during the INC-led UPA, made recommendations on notice, compensation and union recognition; the 2019-2020 bills and 2025 notification came under BJP-led governments [3][8][9]. Congress-led states such as Karnataka and Rajasthan have passed their own worker-protection laws, and BJP-led Uttar Pradesh has issued its own rules [1][6].

What is measured is limited. Most effects reported so far are projections, and the status of state rules differs between sources. The positions and claims below are attributed so that each can be traced; the page does not recommend an option.
